Speaker A: I've been dancing ballet since I was six, and this September I finally passed the entrance exam to the Moscow State Academy of Choreography — one of only twelve girls chosen out of nearly two hundred applicants. My days are now split between academic subjects in the morning and four hours of pointe work in the afternoon, and by the time I get home I can barely feel my toes. My friends outside dance think I'm a bit crazy, but on stage every muscle finally makes sense, and I honestly can't picture my future without the smell of resin and the sound of a live orchestra.

Speaker B: Every August my family disappears from the city and heads out to a small settlement on the shores of Lake Baikal. We take the overnight train from Irkutsk, then a wooden boat piled with tents and cooking pots, and we live for a fortnight without electricity or reliable mobile signal. It sounds hard, but it's the only place on earth where the silence has a physical weight — no traffic, no notifications, just the wind, the ripple of freshwater against the rocks and, at dawn, the occasional call of a nerpa seal. Those two weeks reset something inside me that Moscow can't fix.

Speaker C: Almost every Sunday I catch the metro to the Tretyakov Gallery, where my mother has worked as a guide since before I was born. Growing up between Serov's portraits and Vasnetsov's fairy-tale panels, I learned to read a canvas before I learned to read a book, and by ten I already knew which brush strokes belonged to whom. I've decided to study art history at university and, if things go well, to train as a museum curator specialising in the Russian silver age. My mother pretends she isn't influencing me, but I know she's secretly thrilled.

Speaker D: After school I devote at least three hours a day to online chess — not just casual games but proper analysis with a coach in Saint Petersburg who follows my openings via video call. My Elo rating has just crossed 1930, which is high enough to qualify me for the junior regional championship in Kazan next month, and if I place in the top three I get a place at the national training camp. People still think chess isn't a sport, but after a five-hour tournament your back aches, your eyes burn and you sleep for twelve hours straight — believe me, it counts.

Speaker E: I've supported Spartak Moscow since I was seven, when my grandfather first took me to the old stadium on a freezing October evening — I still remember the smell of grilled sausages and the roar of forty thousand voices around us. Now we go together at least once a month, and even though he can hardly climb the stairs to our seats any more, he refuses to give up his season ticket. Some of my classmates prefer basketball or online games, but for us, football is really about the two of us: three generations of Spartak scarves in one family, and a hundred stories on the way home.

Speaker F: Baking has become the thing I turn to whenever school gets too much — my grandmother taught me the family recipes when I was still standing on a stool to reach the counter. We make kulich cake for Easter with candied fruit and saffron, tall stacks of blini for Maslenitsa served with sour cream and salted red caviar, honey cake soaked overnight for birthdays, and gingerbread pryaniki for New Year's Eve. There's something almost meditative about kneading dough and watching yeast come alive, and my kitchen slowly filling with the smell of vanilla is honestly better than any therapy I could afford.

Reading 1 · 7 iconic Russian places · match texts to titlesEGE 10 · Б · 3 points

7 dense B2 texts · 8 titles · one is EXTRA · themes: nature · engineering · power · ballet · sport · art · region · answers shuffled
A Formed some twenty-five million years ago and set deep in the mountains of southern Siberia, this crescent-shaped body of water holds around one-fifth of all unfrozen fresh water on the planet. Local people, whose ancestors were Buryat shamans, still call it the sacred sea. It shelters an extraordinary collection of endemic species — from the transparent shrimp that filters its water to the nerpa, the only exclusively freshwater seal in the world. In late winter, when the surface freezes into ice so clear that you can watch fish swim beneath your boots, hundreds of skaters and photographers make the long trip from Irkutsk to see it.
B Stretching for nine thousand two hundred and eighty-eight kilometres — the longest continuous railway line on the planet — it was ordered by Alexander III and finished, at colossal human cost, under his son Nicholas II. Its rails cross eight time zones, seven federal districts and virtually every possible landscape, from marshy taiga north of Krasnoyarsk to the Ural passes and, finally, the port hills of Vladivostok. To this day, ordinary travellers who buy a platzkart ticket end up sharing tea and family stories with complete strangers for six or seven nights on end.
C Encircled by fortified red-brick walls that have withstood fire, invasion and revolution, this cluster of gold-domed cathedrals, senate halls and squat towers has served as the operational headquarters of Russian power for more than five hundred years. Ivan the Terrible was crowned inside its Assumption Cathedral, Napoleon watched Moscow burn from its ramparts, and the twenty-first-century presidents still walk to work through the same medieval gates. Even the enormous Tsar Bell, cast in 1735 and never rung, lies quietly beside the Ivan the Great Bell Tower, as if waiting for its cue.
D Founded in 1776 and rebuilt after a devastating fire in the reign of Alexander II, this Moscow theatre gave the world Marius Petipa's choreography, Tchaikovsky's Swan Lake and, later, the defection of Rudolf Nureyev. Behind its heavy gold curtain, a company of some two hundred dancers still trains twelve hours a day, and any ticket to an opening night — even in the highest gallery — is considered an event on its own. To grow up on this stage is to be reminded, every evening, that Russia has exported ballet as vigorously as any of its natural resources.
E A subtropical resort city where palm trees line the promenade and the Black Sea rarely falls below plus five in January, it stunned the world in 2014 by hosting the Winter Olympic Games. Downhill skiers competed in the freshly-carved slopes of Krasnaya Polyana, forty minutes up the mountain, while figure skaters performed at sea level beneath waving palms. The Games left a legacy of high-speed rail, four-star hotels and, more controversially, one of the most expensive infrastructure bills in Olympic history. Today, both Russian and international tourists come year-round — for skiing in February and swimming in July, sometimes in the same week.
F Originally the private art collection of Catherine the Great, opened up to the public only in 1852, this green-and-white palace on the Neva now houses more than three million objects — from Rembrandt's return of the prodigal son to Egyptian sarcophagi and Matisse's dance. Its Winter Palace section alone contains almost four hundred rooms, and a curious visitor could spend, on average, nine seconds at each exhibit and still need two full years to see it all. Meanwhile, some seventy cats descended from those brought in by Elizabeth Petrovna to hunt rats still officially patrol the basement corridors.
G A thousand-year-old city on the Volga where the pale-blue minarets of the Kul Sharif Mosque and the golden crosses of the Annunciation Cathedral share the same fortified skyline, and every street sign is written in two alphabets. Once the medieval capital of the Tatar Khanate, and later a proud showcase of the 2013 Summer Universiade, it is now one of the most economically dynamic cities of central Russia. Ask any local student what makes their city different, and the answer usually comes with pride: here, two civilisations don't just meet — they cook, work and pray together.

Reading 3 · Dmitri Mendeleev · full understandingEGE 12–18 · В · 7 points

1 text · 7 multiple-choice items · not tsars — science this time

Dmitri Mendeleev was born in 1834 in the small Siberian town of Tobolsk, the youngest of seventeen children. When he was thirteen, his father died and his mother took him thousands of kilometres by horse to Moscow, and then to Saint Petersburg, so that he could receive a proper education. Later Mendeleev would say that everything he became, he owed to that single stubborn journey.

At university he specialised in chemistry, but he was equally interested in agriculture, oil refining and even balloon flight. In 1869, at the age of thirty-five, he did the work that made him world-famous: he arranged all sixty-three known chemical elements into a single table, ordered by atomic weight. What impressed the scientific world most was not the table itself — several other chemists had tried something similar — but the fact that Mendeleev left empty squares, predicting elements that had not yet been discovered. Within twenty years, gallium, scandium and germanium had appeared, exactly where he said they would.

Mendeleev's public life was not always easy. He argued with government ministers, he was refused a professorship in Saint Petersburg for being «too democratic», and his second marriage created a scandal because the Church had not yet approved the divorce from his first wife. But he stayed a working scientist to the end. His last big project was to modernise the Russian system of weights and measures — a boring administrative job that ordinary chemists would have avoided.

Today, more than one hundred and fifty years after his table, it hangs in every chemistry classroom in the world. And curiously, in Russia itself, Mendeleev is remembered not only for chemistry but for his belief that a scientist has a duty to improve everyday life — from the size of a school desk to the quality of vodka.

Speaking 1 · Read the text aloud · 3 paragraphsEGE устная 1 · Б · 1 point

1.5 min silent prep · then read aloud · up to 5 phonetic mistakes accepted · dense B2 popular-science text

Watch the highlighted words — they carry the meaning & the phonetic traps.

On the twelfth of April 1961, a twenty-seven-year-old test pilot with a peasant childhood in a small village near Smolensk climbed into a tiny spherical capsule called Vostok One. His name was Yuri Gagarin, and although he had been carefully rehearsed for every possible emergency, the whole flight itself lasted only one hundred and eight minutes — just one full orbit around our planet.

Everything Gagarin observed during that single lap was completely unprecedented. He was the first human being to see the round horizon of Earth, the thin blue line of the atmosphere and the deep black of open space. His radio commentary was calm, almost poetic: he described the continents as they slid beneath him, and famously exclaimed «Poyekhali!» — «Let's go!» — the moment the boosters ignited.

After his return, Gagarin was transformed overnight into a global celebrity, meeting Queen Elizabeth the Second and travelling to more than thirty countries as the symbol of the Soviet space programme. Yet he never lost his interest in flying and, tragically, died just seven years later during a routine training exercise. Today, every Russian schoolchild can quote his opening word, and the twelfth of April is still marked as Cosmonautics Day — a national moment of pride and remembrance.

Hi! I've found two great photos for our project on «Ways of Exploring Russian Cities», and I'd like to share my thoughts on them. In the first photo, we can see a young tourist standing on Red Square. She has a backpack and headphones — clearly she is exploring Moscow on her own with an audio guide. The second photo, on the other hand, shows a small group of tourists in Saint Petersburg, listening to a professional guide who is telling them about the Church on Spilled Blood. The main difference is obvious: the first tourist is alone and independent, while the second group depends on a real person. Speaking of advantages, an audio guide gives you total freedom — you can stop, replay, or skip anything, and you can move at your own pace. A professional guide, however, offers something an app cannot: personal stories, answers to your questions, and a route built by someone who knows the city. Of course, both ways have drawbacks too. With an audio guide, you can feel lonely and you may miss context that is not in the recording. With a professional guide, you have to follow the group's pace, and a bad guide can spoil the whole day. Personally, I would prefer a professional guide, especially in a city with a rich history like Saint Petersburg. A living voice gives history colour that no recording can. To sum up, both formats work — but for me, a guided walk beats a headphone tour every time.
