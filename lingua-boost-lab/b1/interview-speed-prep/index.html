Warm-up · five simple questions about youSpeak first

Soft start. Read each question, then speak your own answer aloud — full sentences, no rush. By question 5 your mouth is in English mode again.

These are the questions every conversation opens with. Don't memorise an answer — just say each one out loud in your own words. The line underneath is a sample structure — replace every blank with your own detail.

Question 1 · who you are
Tell me a little about yourself.
"I'm a lawyer specialising in Russian law. I have around ___ years of experience, mainly in ___."
Question 2 · what you do
What do you do — what's your role?
"In my current role, I mainly handle ___, review ___ and advise ___."
Question 3 · your day
Walk me through a typical day at work.
"A typical day usually mixes ___, ___, and ___. The priorities can change quickly."
Question 4 · your English
How long have you been learning English?
"I have studied English for ___. Most of my daily legal work is conducted in Russian, so I'm now focusing on building confidence in professional English communication."
Question 5 · what's keeping you busy
What's been keeping you busy recently?
"Recently I have been working on ___. It has required ___ — intense but interesting."
Sample answer — replace every detail with your own. The blanks aren't decoration — fill them with your real experience before you speak. Possible areas: commercial law / corporate law / employment law / compliance / contracts / litigation / regulatory matters / tax law.

By now you should feel looser. The hard stuff comes next, but you are warmed up.

Three openings that sound mature and confident. Use them when you answer. Keep "well…" and "you know" rare — overused, they make you sound junior.

2

Rescue phrases · when you don't understand or forget a wordMost important block

Memorise four of these. Say them with calm voice — and you never look bad. These phrases are common in professional interviews and help you stay composed.

Your real fear is not the questions — it's the moment you don't catch a word or can't find one in English. These phrases buy you 2-3 seconds and keep your composure. They are the most professional thing you can say.

When you didn't catch the question

"Sorry, could you rephrase that, please?"
"Just to make sure I understand correctly — are you asking about my experience with international clients?"
"Could you give me a moment to think about that?"
"That's a very good question, thank you. Let me think."

When you forgot a word in English

"What's the word in English… in legal practice we call this…"
"I can't find the exact term in English right now, but in Russian law we'd refer to it as…"
"Bear with me, I'm reaching for the precise English word."
"Let me put it differently…"

When you need to think · filler phrases that sound professional

"That's an interesting angle. From my perspective…"
"Speaking from my experience…"
"The honest answer is…"
"The way I see it…"
Avoid overusing
  • A bare "I don't know" is fine if you follow it up: "I haven't dealt with this directly, but my approach would be…"
  • Constant "um", "well", "you know" — used in every sentence, they flatten you. A single "well" is fine. Replace the rest with a pause. Silence is mature.
  • "Maybe", "perhaps", "I think" in every sentence — replace with "In my view…", "From what I've seen…"

6 real situations · which rescue to use

Don't memorise the phrases. Recognise the situation — the right phrase will come.

Situation 1
The director asks about an area you haven't worked in directly.
↪ "I don't have direct experience with that, but my first step would be to clarify the legal and business context, identify the relevant jurisdiction, and involve the appropriate local experts."
Situation 2
The director uses a long sentence with three legal terms. You catch only two. You're not sure what is being asked.
↪ "Just to make sure I understand correctly — are you asking about how I handle regulatory pressure during a transaction?"
Situation 3
You know the answer perfectly in Russian. You blank on the English word for "налоговая проверка".
↪ "In Russian law we'd refer to this as a tax audit — bear with me one second — yes, a tax audit. Let me tell you how I handled the last one."
Situation 4
A behavioural question hits you cold. You need three seconds to structure a STAR.
↪ "That's a very good question, thank you. Let me think for a moment so I give you a complete example."
Situation 5
You're answering and you realise your first sentence was unclear. Restart cleanly.
↪ "Let me put it differently. The way I see it, my role is to translate complex Russian regulation into business decisions the international team can act on."
Situation 6
You've been talking for over a minute. You notice the director's attention start to drift. Wrap up gracefully.
↪ "In a nutshell — the case settled, the client stayed, and I learned to push for early structured negotiation. Would you like me to elaborate on any part?"

"My memos are short and structured: key findings up front, two or three options with recommendations, and clear next steps."

Top 5 questions · with model answersCultural-fit interview

For each question · listen → say YOUR version → polish wording. The model is a starting frame, not a script to memorise.
Question 1 · Opening

Director: Tell me a little about yourself.

You: Thank you. I'm a lawyer specialising in Russian law. I have around ___ years of professional experience. In my current role, I mainly handle ___ and advise clients or colleagues on ___. What attracts me to this opportunity is the chance to use my legal expertise in an international business environment.

Question 2 · Motivation

Director: Why our company? What attracts you?

You: What attracts me is the opportunity to work in an international environment while applying my expertise in Russian law. I'm also interested in understanding how the local legal team works with European management and other international stakeholders. The position seems to combine legal expertise, business judgement and international communication — a combination I find particularly attractive.

Question 3 · Working style with an international leader

Director: How do you work with a manager who is based in another country?

You: I believe successful international cooperation depends on clear communication, realistic deadlines and regular updates. I usually try to explain the context, identify the main risk and suggest a practical next step. I'm also comfortable asking clarifying questions when expectations are not completely clear.

Question 4 · A difficult moment Sample structure · do not memorise

Director: Tell me about a difficult professional situation and how you handled it.

You (STAR): A good example was when we faced a sudden change that affected an ongoing matter. The Situation was that we were in the middle of advising the client. My Task was to assess the impact and recommend the right next step. The Action I took was to map the legal risks against the business priorities and prepare a memo with two or three clear options. The Result was that the client made an informed decision quickly, and the working relationship became stronger because we had been honest about the trade-offs.

Question 5 · Area you are developing

Director: What is one area you are actively working to improve?

You: One area I am actively developing is concise communication in English. Because legal issues can be complex, I want to explain them clearly without unnecessary detail. I have been practising this deliberately, and I already feel more confident structuring my answers and focusing on the key point.

5

STAR method · how senior people tell storiesBehavioural questions

Any question starting with "Tell me about a time…" or "Give me an example…" wants STAR.

S — T — A — R · four short sentences

S — Situation set the scene in one sentence T — Task what was YOUR responsibility A — Action what YOU specifically did (use "I", not "we") R — Result numbers or concrete outcome
Worked example · "Tell me about a difficult negotiation"

Director: Tell me about a difficult negotiation.

You: A good example was a settlement negotiation last spring. Situation — a long-running contract dispute was about to go to court. Task — find a settlement before the hearing, because litigation would damage a key client relationship. Action — I prepared a structured offer in three layers (what we would concede, what we would not, what we needed in writing) and I led two rounds of meetings with the other side. Result — we settled within a week, the client kept the relationship, and we billed two-thirds less than a full trial would have cost.

Three behavioural prompts to prepare BEFORE the interview
1. A difficult colleague or stakeholder. What was the conflict, what did you do, what changed?
2. A high-pressure deadline. What was the deal, how did you protect quality, what was the outcome?
3. A case where you went outside your comfort zone. What new area, what did you learn, how do you use it now?
Your turn · template

Pick one of the three prompts above. Build YOUR story in four sentences.

Situation: ____ (one sentence — the context) Task: ____ (one sentence — what was YOUR responsibility) Action: I ____ (one sentence — what you specifically did, in I-form) Result: ____ (one sentence — the outcome, with a number or a relationship change)
Say it once slowly to lock the shape, then again at natural pace. Avoid the trap of "we" — interviewers want to hear YOUR contribution.
6

Your questions back · the closerShowing seniority

When the director says "Do you have any questions for me?" — never say "No". Ask two. Strong candidates close the interview with their own questions.
Three smart back-questions · pick TWO for tomorrow

You (about the role): "If you imagine someone doing brilliantly in this role twelve months from now, what would they have delivered?"

Why it works: shows you think in outcomes, not in tasks. Senior frame.

You (about the team): "How does the international team typically work with Russian counsel — closely, as advisors, or more independently?"

Why it works: shows you've thought about the cross-border dynamic and you care about fit, not just the title.

You (about the director, classy closer): "What do you personally enjoy most about leading this team from where you sit?"

Why it works: humanises the conversation, lets the director talk about themselves — interviewers leave happy when they get to share.

The polite closing — exactly what to say at the end

One short final sentence. Practice it three times. This is your last impression.

Thank you very much for the conversation today. ____ (One specific reflection — what you found genuinely interesting in this conversation). I would be delighted to take the next step, and please let me know if there is anything else you need from my side.
Avoid "I'm very interested" — every candidate says that. Replace with a real, specific sentence about what the director said.

Thank-you email · within 24 hoursOptional · senior signal

Optional — but a polite follow-up within 24 hours is a strong senior signal. Three short paragraphs.

Send this to your recruiter within 24 hours of the interview, asking them to forward it to the director. Or directly to the director if you have their email. Keep it short. Keep it specific. One concrete reference to your conversation = senior signal.

Copy-and-edit template

Subject: Thank you for our conversation today Dear [Director's name], Thank you very much for your time today. I really enjoyed our conversation, and I particularly appreciated ____ (one specific thing the director said — the company's approach to / how you described / what you mentioned about ___). The role excites me because ____ (one specific connection between your strengths and the work), and I left the conversation feeling that ____ (one honest observation — the team / the culture / the platform) would be a strong match. I would be delighted to take the next step. If there is anything else you need from my side, please let me know. With kind regards, [Your name] [Phone] [LinkedIn]
Write it tonight, before bed. Send it within 24 hours, ideally during the recipient's working hours.
Small things that make it senior
  • Use "with kind regards" or "warm regards", not "best" or "thanks"
  • Spell their name correctly — copy it from LinkedIn, not memory
  • One sentence about the WORK, not just "thank you" + signature
  • No exclamation marks. None.
