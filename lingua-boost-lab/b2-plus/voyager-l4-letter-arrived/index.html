03

Reading · The night the letter arrived

Reyes Lab · field journal · 23 April · 03:17

The night nurse had left at midnight. The envelope on Theo's bedside table had not moved since. It was addressed in his own handwriting — but he had never owned a pen since the stroke, and no one in the building had seen him form a letter in three years. The address read, in slow careful capitals: To the visitor in chair 2 — by hand.

I unsealed it for him, because he could not. The paper inside was the letter Dr Reyes had drafted for her team at the end of last week — the one she had asked us to copy and send to anyone who had ever doubted her. He had heard it read aloud once already, on Tuesday. Tonight he wanted, I think, to write it down himself, blink by blink, in a way that made him the author and not the witness.

I read the letter to him a third time. I had been told, by Dr Reyes, that he had nothing left to hide — that he had said everything he could say in eleven weeks of three short bursts and two long ones. I had been told to expect an admission, not surprise.

"Not once in three years had he opened his eyes wider than a normal blink. Tonight, halfway through paragraph two, he opened them as if he were trying to see who I was."

— Dr Reyes, that night, on the tape

Halfway through paragraph two, he blinked six letters at me, one at a time. F · A · T · H · E · R.

I am thirty-one years old. I have been Dr Naomi Reyes for the last nine of those years. The other twenty-two I was Naomi Marshall. My father walked out of our house in March of 1995 — the same March that, exactly twenty-eight years later, would take his body and leave him here. I had not told Theo I had found him. I had not told the lab he was my father. I had hidden the truth from my own case, and now my own patient was telling me he had known the whole time.

The clock on the wall — the round brass one that had hung over his bed since he arrived, that I had never asked about — read 03:17. Forty-one days after I had gone public with his signal. Twenty-eight years and one month after the night he had left. I looked at it and remembered, only then, that it was the same clock my mother had owned. I had been reading the timeline from the front of the case. He had been winding it from the back.

I sat down in chair 2.

04

Listening · the press conference, day 42

A 3-minute clip from Dr Reyes' second press conference — the one she gave the morning after the letter arrived. She is asked, on camera, about a conflict of interest. Listen for what she says and what she admits she should have said earlier.

Journalist: Dr Reyes, you released the Marshall data forty-one days ago. Last night something changed in this case. Can you confirm what?

Reyes: I can. Theodore Marshall is my father. I have not lived with him since I was three. I changed my surname when I was twenty-two. I should have told this room on day one. I didn't.

Journalist: Why didn't you?

Reyes: Because I was afraid the science would be dismissed as a daughter's grief project. I now believe I was wrong about that — and that the delay caused harm. I'm sorry for that delay, particularly to the patient himself.

Journalist: Some will say the data should be reviewed by a lab with no family tie. Will you accept that?

Reyes: I have already asked Goldberg's group at McGill to re-analyse every blink in the record. They have the raw files as of this morning. If they confirm what we have, the discovery stands; if they don't, we retract.

Journalist: Has your father — has Theo — spoken about you, in his blinks?

Reyes: He blinked one word at me, two nights ago. Just one. I won't repeat it in this room. It was a private letter, and it's the only one he could send.

Journalist: Last question — what should we have understood about this case from the start, that we didn't?

Reyes: That patients like Theo could have been heard a decade ago, if we had asked. Never had any of us imagined how much had been said in those rooms — only that no one was writing it down.

Q1 — The right to be heard, after forty-one days of silence. My short answer is: she kept the right by going public on day 42 — but only because she went without being forced. The reason I defend that is procedural: science doesn't ask whether a result was discovered with clean hands, it asks whether the data survives independent re-analysis. She has already sent every blink to McGill; if Goldberg's group confirms the signal, her silence about the family link is a serious ethics issue, but it doesn't unbuild what the patient was telling her. The strongest objection from the other side is that a forty-one-day silence about a daughter–father tie is itself data — that her interpretation of his blinks might have been generous. The condition under which I would reverse my view is precisely that: if any of the eleven decoded weeks turns out to be over-reading, she goes. Q2 — Six letters to someone you have not spoken to. I would send them to my grandmother, who has been dead nine years. The six letters would be «I HEARD.» Two words, one of them only one letter. I am deliberately not writing what I heard — because writing it down would let me edit it, and what I want her to know is that the version of her she left me, the raw one, is the one that stayed. What I fear she would read into it is that I am thanking her too late. What I want her to read into it is that there is no such thing as too late when the listening was done in time. Q3 — Breakfast with Naomi the morning after FATHER. About her work, I would say: publish the science exactly as you would have published it before yesterday — keep the lab's hands clean of the family story, so the family story doesn't pollute the data. About her father, I would say: he has been answering you for three years; the fact that you only just heard the word doesn't mean he started saying it last night. The part I would tell her to keep private is the six letters themselves — those are not for the press, they are for her. The sentence I would not say out loud, because she already knows it: he forgave you before you forgave him. CHALLENGE A — Devil's advocate (opposite to Q1). Suppose I argued the opposite — that Reyes has lost the right to be heard as a scientist regardless of day-42 disclosure. Her critics have insisted that any interpretation of an EEG by a relative is no longer interpretation, but advocacy. They have maintained, with some force, that she had ample weeks to declare and didn't, and that scientists who control disclosure also control narrative. Her supporters have conceded that the delay was wrong while arguing the science still stands on its own. The honest middle is that the science is intact but the storytelling is compromised. In fact, what she has lost is not the right to be heard, but the right to be the only voice in the room. CHALLENGE B — Theo's ninety seconds. Naomi. I had been telling you, since March of nineteen ninety-five, that I should have come back. The week your mother put my watch in your drawer was the week I started to. I lost the bus, and then I lost the year, and then I lost the door — and after that there was nothing to come back to that you would still answer. I should have written. I should have phoned. I should have shown up at school. Instead I read about you for fifteen years, in journals, and I was proud, and I was a coward, and the two are not the same word but I made them the same answer. Never had I imagined I would talk to you only after I lost my voice. Keep doing the work. Stop reading my drafts. Theo, on the record, says: you were always the patient I came back for.

Episode 4 of 4 · series closing
