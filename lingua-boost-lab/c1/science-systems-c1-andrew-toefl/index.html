01

Warm-up · think before you read

Three formats — open questions, agree/disagree, true/false. Take a position out loud, then justify it. Whole section → homework.

A · Eight open questions · 30 seconds each

  1. What field of science do you find the most interesting? Why?
  2. Which science is more useful in everyday life — biology, chemistry or physics?
  3. Can science solve most global problems, or are some problems beyond science?
  4. What scientific discovery has changed human life the most?
  5. Why do people sometimes distrust science?
  6. Should governments spend more money on space exploration or medicine?
  7. How can science be harmful if it is used irresponsibly?
  8. Which is more important for a scientist — creativity, knowledge or patience? Why?
C1 speaking core. Reach for these phrases in every answer: this field focuses on… · it plays a crucial role in… · one major concern is… · this may lead to… · from a long-term perspective… · the main benefit is that… · it is crucial to strike a balance between innovation and responsibility.

B · Agree or disagree · pick your side, then defend it for 30 seconds

1. Science is the most reliable way of understanding the world.
2. Artificial intelligence will eventually replace human scientists in most research areas.
3. Genetic engineering of human embryos should be banned worldwide.
4. Nuclear energy is a necessary part of the future energy mix.
5. Funding space exploration is harder to justify than funding medical research.
6. Scientific publications should be free for everyone, with no paywalls.
7. A breakthrough that has a small risk of catastrophic harm should still be pursued.
8. Scientists carry a moral responsibility for how their discoveries are used.

C · True or false · separate myth from fact about science

1. Humans only use about 10% of their brain — the rest is dormant.
→ False. Neuroimaging shows virtually every region is active across a 24-hour cycle. The "10%" claim is a long-standing myth with no scientific basis.
2. Antibiotics have no effect on viral infections such as the common cold or flu.
→ True. Antibiotics target bacterial cell walls and ribosomes. Viruses use host-cell machinery and are unaffected — overuse drives resistance without benefit.
3. Lightning never strikes the same place twice.
→ False. Tall isolated structures attract repeat strikes — the Empire State Building is hit roughly 20–25 times every year.
4. A scientific theory is the strongest form of explanation in science, not a guess.
→ True. A theory unites a wide body of evidence, predicts new findings and survives repeated testing (relativity, evolution, plate tectonics). In everyday English "theory" means guess — in science it does not.
5. The Great Wall of China is the only human-made structure visible from space with the naked eye.
→ False. Astronauts have repeatedly stated the wall is essentially invisible from low Earth orbit, let alone from the Moon — it is too narrow.
6. mRNA vaccine technology was researched for decades before COVID-19, not invented in 2020.
→ True. The foundational work on modified-nucleoside mRNA dates back to the early 2000s. COVID-19 accelerated the deployment, not the discovery.
7. Quantum computers will make all current encryption useless within the next five years.
→ False. Current quantum systems are far from breaking standard encryption at scale. The concern is real but long-horizon — post-quantum cryptography is already being standardised in preparation.
8. Adult human brains keep producing new neurons in certain regions throughout life.
→ True. Neurogenesis has been confirmed in the hippocampus and olfactory bulb of adults. The older "neurons stop forming after childhood" claim is outdated.

11e

Writing · Academic Discussion · TOEFL Task 2 prep

Take a clear position. Give two reasons, each with one concrete example. Address one counterargument. Aim for 180–250 words, C1 academic register. Whole section → homework.

Professor's prompt

"Should governments require AI developers to publish the data used to train large language models? Take a clear position. Support it with two reasons and address one objection from the opposite side."

Words · 0 Target · 180–250
In my view, governments should require developers of large language models to publish at least a structured summary of their training data, even if not every individual document. The case for mandatory disclosure rests on two pillars. First, transparency is the most reliable safeguard against systemic bias. When the dataset is hidden, no external researcher can verify whether the model under-represents particular populations or amplifies questionable sources. The recent controversy over a major chatbot reproducing copyrighted text is a clear illustration of why opacity carries reputational and legal risk for everyone involved. Second, publication accelerates legitimate research. Academic groups studying alignment or fairness currently rely on indirect probes precisely because the underlying data is proprietary. A structured disclosure regime — similar to how pharmaceutical trials publish protocols — would let independent scientists replicate findings without exposing trade secrets. The strongest objection is that full disclosure could enable harmful exploitation of personal information. That concern is real, but it argues for tiered disclosure rather than blanket secrecy: aggregated statistics in public, fine-grained metadata under audited access. Overall, the responsibility that comes with deploying such powerful systems is incompatible with treating their training data as a black box.

Model C1 answers — read silently as scaffolds

Four short C1-band model answers. Read each one silently, then say the same idea in your own words. Do not memorise. Whole section → homework.

1 · Nuclear energy — why opinions remain divided

Opinions remain divided because nuclear energy combines major advantages with potentially catastrophic risks. Supporters emphasise low carbon emissions and continuous, weather-independent output, while opponents focus on accidents, radioactive waste, and high construction costs. Historical disasters such as Chernobyl and Fukushima continue to shape public opinion both emotionally and politically. As a result, debates about nuclear power almost always blend scientific evidence with social fear — and this combination makes consensus extremely difficult.

2 · Materials science — how biodegradable polymers help

Biodegradable polymers reduce environmental impact because they decompose under specific natural conditions, unlike traditional plastics that remain in ecosystems for decades. Modern packaging derived from plant-based polymers already breaks down more efficiently in landfills and oceans. This innovation could significantly improve sustainability across manufacturing and waste management. However, durability and production costs remain serious challenges, so researchers continue searching for the right balance between ecology and economic practicality.

3 · Neuroscience — why people rely on emotion in stressful moments

People rely on emotion because emotional responses are much faster than analytical reasoning. In stressful or uncertain situations, the brain prioritises quick reactions over careful analysis — a survival mechanism that still operates in modern life. For instance, during financial crises many investors panic and sell impulsively, even when the data suggests that staying calm would be rational. Emotions are not weakness; they are an evolutionary shortcut, but the same shortcut can mislead us in environments evolution never anticipated.

4 · Zoology — why endangered species are hard to protect

Endangered species are difficult to protect because their survival depends on several interconnected factors: habitat protection, conservation policy, human behaviour and global environmental conditions. Even if one area is rigorously protected, illegal hunting or climate change can still threaten the same species elsewhere. For example, the Amur leopard remains critically endangered partly because protected reserves cannot fully prevent poaching beyond their borders. From a broader perspective, real protection requires coordinated international effort rather than isolated local action.
